Context

A platform for social entrepreneurs that nobody could explain.

Braid started at the University of Michigan as PIT-KN — the Public Interest Technology Knowledge Network. It was built so social entrepreneurs and founders working on civic-tech problems could share the honest highs and lows of their journeys.

The substance was real. The framing wasn't. The homepage talked about "knowledge networks" and "public interest technology" — language that made sense to insiders and meant nothing to a first-time visitor. People landed, didn't see themselves, and left.

I joined as Product Designer and UX Researcher, leading the research pass that diagnosed the problem and the redesign that fixed it. The work also folded in data-equity considerations for the community: many of the entrepreneurs the platform exists for come from communities whose stories have historically been extracted, not amplified.

Problem

New visitors couldn't tell what this thing was in 5 seconds.

First interviews surfaced the same pattern over and over: visitors arrived, scanned the homepage, and left without a single click into the actual stories. The product worked for people who already knew what it was. It failed at first contact.

"I grappled with understanding its purpose beyond being a mere 'knowledge network.'"

— User interview participant

That quote became the brief. The redesign wasn't a visual refresh — it was a translation problem.

Research

Generative and evaluative work across 100+ stakeholders.

I directed both halves of the research stack — generative work to find the gap, evaluative work to prove the fix landed. The community side coordinated with 100+ members: founders, alumni, organizers, and academics whose work the platform represents.

Insight 1
Stickiness came from specific founder stories, not from the platform's abstract framing. People stayed for narrative, not taxonomy.
Insight 2
First-time visitors couldn't translate "public interest technology" into a self-relevant question. The brand was speaking inside-baseball above the fold.
Insight 3
Comparable platforms (Medium, Indie Hackers, On Deck) lead with people and stories, not categorical descriptions of what they are.
Insight 4
The team itself held conflicting mental models of what Braid was supposed to be. Internal alignment was a research finding, not just a design one.

Process

From research insight to a redesigned identity.

01

Generative research

Ran 15+ qualitative interviews with new visitors and community members. Built a centralized insight repository structured so non-technical stakeholders (organizers, strategy leads) could read findings without needing a translator.

02

Competitive & comparative analysis

Audited how peer platforms surfaced value above the fold. Identified the pattern: narrative-first homepages outperformed taxonomy-first ones in this category, every time.

03

Cross-functional synthesis workshops

Facilitated 5+ synthesis workshops with PM, engineering, marketing, and strategy. The goal wasn't agreement on a homepage — it was a shared, written articulation of what Braid was for. The workshops produced the language the redesign would speak.

04

Messaging and visual redesign

Rewrote the homepage to lead with "Your Words. Your Story. Braid." and surfaced real founder stories above the fold. Worked with the head of strategy to design a new mark inspired by the Ife knot — a West African symbol of gathering — that put the community's roots into the identity instead of around it.

05

Evaluative testing & ship

Ran usability tests on prototypes, iterated through weekly standups with engineers and marketers, and validated the new flow with both first-time visitors and existing members before shipping.

Outcome

A 40% lift, and a team that finally agreed on what Braid was.

Within a month of shipping the redesigned homepage and new mark, new sign-ups were up 40%. But the bigger outcome was the part the metric doesn't capture: the team itself was aligned for the first time, on paper, on what the product was for.

The redesign became the artifact that captured that alignment — every later product decision could be cross-checked against the homepage's articulation of purpose. The research repository continued to inform the roadmap after I left.

Takeaways

Three things this project taught me.

A homepage is a research artifact.
The new homepage worked because it was the written output of synthesis workshops, not because it had better visual hierarchy. Copy that holds up under user testing is research compressed into language.
Internal misalignment shows up as bad UX.
When the team can't articulate what a product is for, the homepage can't either. The fastest path to clearer UX in cases like this is structured cross-functional synthesis, not more visual iteration.
Research for marginalized communities means building the repository, not just the report.
For platforms serving communities whose stories have been historically extracted, the insight repository has to be readable by the community itself — not locked behind a research function. Structure matters as much as findings.
